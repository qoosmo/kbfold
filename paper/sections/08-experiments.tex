\section{Experiments}\label{sec:exp}

This section measures the implementation of \cref{sec:impl}.
It has four goals: to check that the kernel encoding costs the same as coefficient-form folding at identical engineering (as predicted by \cref{rem:basefold}), to measure the cost of the post-quantum parameters and of the salted compiler, to locate where the time goes and quantify the protocol parameters $\ell$ and $\rho$, and to place the implementation next to an independent multilinear commitment scheme.

\subsection{Setup}\label{sec:exp:setup}

\paragraph{Machine and code.}
All runs use a cloud virtual machine with an Intel Xeon processor at $2.10$\,GHz ($2$ vCPUs, $7$\,GB RAM), Rust $1.95.0$, release profile with fat LTO and one codegen unit, and no \texttt{target-cpu} flag.
The library is \texttt{kbfold}~\kbversion; the script \texttt{rust/bench/run\_all.sh} runs every measurement of this section and writes the data of the tables to \texttt{rust/results/}.
Unless stated otherwise, runs are single-threaded.
Times are medians over $11$ runs for $n \le 16$, $5$ for $n \in \{18, 20\}$ and $3$ for $n = 22$ and for \cref{tab:stop,tab:rate}; verification times are medians over $21$ runs.
The machine is shared, and timings of identical work vary by up to about $20\%$ between runs (\cref{sec:exp:breakdown}).

\paragraph{Parameters.}
The \emph{classical parameters} are: rate $\rho = 1/4$ ($R = 2$), $\ell = n - 4$ folding rounds (final table of $16$ elements), $\kappa = 148$ queries, the quadratic extension $\K$, $32$-byte salts, and committed levels $\mathcal{J} = \{0, 4, 8, \dots\}$ ($k = 4$, \cref{sec:pcs:skip}).
By \cref{thm:sound} with $\delta = (1-\rho)/2 = 3/8$, the query term is $(5/8)^{148} < 2^{-100}$, and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{fold}} + 2\ell/|\K| < 2^{-100}$ for all sizes tested (\cref{rem:params}).
The \emph{post-quantum parameters} are those of \cref{rem:pq-params,rem:skip-params}: the same rate, $\ell$ and $k$, $\kappa = 248$ queries and the quartic extension.
Tables $f$ are uniformly random over $\mathbb{F}_p$ and the point $z$ is uniformly random in $\F^n$.
Proof sizes are the lengths of the canonical encoding of \cref{sec:impl:merkle}; they depend slightly on the query points, through the number of distinct opened leaves.

\paragraph{Baseline.}
To isolate the effect of the encoding, the library has a second mode that differs from $\Pieval$ in exactly two places: the table is converted to the monomial coefficients of $\tilde f$ by M\"obius inversion (\cref{lem:mobius}) instead of the transform of \cref{cor:transforms}, and words are folded with the classical FRI fold $\mathrm{cfold}_\theta(w) = w_e + \theta\,w_o$ (\cref{def:cfold}) instead of the kernel fold (\cref{rem:coeff-form}).
This is the coefficient-form folding used in Reed--Solomon instantiations of BaseFold and in DeepFold; everything else (field, NTT, Merkle trees, transcript, sumcheck, query phase) is shared.
The baseline passes the same completeness and tampering tests.
It is \emph{not} the BaseFold or DeepFold code base, and \cref{tab:scaling} compares encodings, not implementations.

\Cref{tab:scaling} reports commit, open and verify times for $n = 12,\dots,22$ ($N = 2^{12},\dots,2^{22}$).
Proofs have the same shape in both modes; their sizes differ only through the number of distinct opened leaves.
The differences between the two encodings change sign from one size to the next (the kernel encoding is $9\%$ faster to commit at $n = 18$ and $9\%$ slower at $n = 22$), and they are of the same size as the variation of identical work between runs: the kernel commitment at $n = 20$ takes $610$\,ms in \cref{tab:scaling} and $685$\,ms in the run of \cref{tab:breakdown}.
The two encodings are therefore indistinguishable at the resolution of this machine.
This is the outcome predicted by \cref{rem:basefold}: the kernel transform and M\"obius inversion both cost $\tfrac{n}{2}N$ additions, and in line form (\cref{lem:line}) the kernel fold costs one multiplication by $r$ per output element, the same as the classical fold.
The kernel encoding thus provides its structural property (\cref{thm:fold-dictionary}) at no measurable cost.

\Cref{tab:pq} compares the two parameter sets.
The post-quantum parameters cost $1.3$--$2$ times the prover time and $1.5$--$2.1$ times the proof size of the classical ones: the extension doubles the size of every folded value and of the sumcheck arithmetic, and the number of queries grows from $148$ to $248$.
The commitment does not depend on the parameter set, since $w_0$ lives over $\mathbb{F}_p$ in both cases; the differences in prover time come from opening.

\paragraph{Salts.}
At $n = 20$ with the classical parameters, the prover takes $809$\,ms with unsalted trees and $909$\,ms with $32$-byte salts.
Salts add $49$\,KiB to the proof ($187$ to $236$\,KiB): one salt per distinct opened leaf (up to $8$ fibres per query in the commitment tree and one coset per query in each of the three other trees) and $17$ round salts.

\paragraph{Parallelism.}
With the feature \texttt{parallel}, the transforms, the NTT, the Merkle trees, the folds and the sumcheck run on all cores; the proofs are identical to the single-threaded ones, which the test vectors check.
On the two vCPUs of this machine, the prover time with the classical parameters drops from $842$ to $632$\,ms at $n = 20$ and from $4631$ to $2920$\,ms at $n = 22$ (speed-ups $1.3$ and $1.6$), and with the post-quantum parameters from $1319$ to $965$\,ms and from $5916$ to $4263$\,ms ($1.4$ both).
The verifier stays single-threaded.

\label{tab:breakdown}
\end{table}

\Cref{tab:breakdown} splits the commitment into its three steps.
The kernel transform takes $2\%$ of the commitment, as long as M\"obius inversion: both perform $\tfrac{n}{2}N$ additions.
The commitment is dominated by hashing: BLAKE3 over $M/2$ salted fibre leaves and $M/2 - 1$ internal nodes takes twice as long as the NTT.
Opening costs about a third of committing: the prover folds every word, but builds trees only for the committed words $w_4, w_8, w_{12}$, whose leaves hold $16$ values each; the sumcheck ($O(N)$ multiplications in $\F$) is a minor term.

Verification takes $0.5$--$1.4$\,ms with the classical parameters and grows slowly with $n$: each query opens four leaves, and the merged paths share their upper nodes.
Proofs take $80$--$290$\,KiB; at $n = 20$, about $44\%$ is opened values, $20\%$ salts and $35\%$ path nodes.

\Cref{tab:stop} varies $\ell$ at $n = 20$.
With committed levels, a folding round adds a committed word only every $k = 4$ rounds, so proof sizes vary little with $\ell$ ($218$--$241$\,KiB); stopping earlier adds $16 N_\ell$ bytes for the final table, which dominates at $\ell = 8$.

\Cref{tab:arity} varies the number $k$ of levels between committed words.
With $k = 1$ ($\Pieval$), merging the paths alone gives $556$\,KiB, against $1147$\,KiB with one authentication path per query and level.
Committing to one word in $k$ divides the number of opened leaves by about $k$, at the cost of $2^k$ values per leaf; the proof shrinks by a factor $1.9$ at $k = 2$ and $2.3$--$2.4$ at $k = 3$ and $4$, where the larger leaves offset the fewer paths.
The prover gets faster because it builds fewer trees, and the verifier because it hashes fewer paths.
We take $k = 4$, which gives the fastest verifier.

WHIR~\cite{ACFY24} is a multilinear polynomial commitment scheme from Reed--Solomon proximity testing.
To place our implementation among existing systems, \cref{tab:whir} runs its implementation\footnote{Repository \url{https://github.com/WizardOfMenlo/whir}, commit \texttt{c03a4a5} of September 2026.} on the same machine with the same field, rate and security level, single-threaded, and without proof of work.
We use two configurations: folding one variable per round with the unique-decoding analysis, and WHIR's default of four variables per round with its analysis up to the Johnson bound, the configuration closest to ours in the number of committed words.
The two code bases differ in many engineering choices (field arithmetic, Merkle trees, transcript, proof encoding), so \cref{tab:whir} is a reference point, not a comparison of protocols.

On this machine, \texttt{kbfold} with the classical parameters has a prover $6.6$--$9$ times faster than WHIR with one variable per round, with proofs $1.9$--$2.3$ times smaller and a verifier $3$--$4$ times faster.
Against WHIR's default configuration, the \texttt{kbfold} prover is $1.8$--$3.1$ times faster, while WHIR's proofs are $1.9$--$2.4$ times smaller and its verifier $1.6$--$2.1$ times faster.
Both schemes commit to one word in four; the remaining difference in proof size and verification time comes from the number of queries, which WHIR reduces by its analysis up to the Johnson bound.
The list-decoding extension of \cref{thm:sound} in \cref{sec:concl:future} addresses exactly this term, and it is independent of the encoding.
