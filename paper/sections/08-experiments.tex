\section{Experiments}\label{sec:exp}

This section measures the implementation of \cref{sec:impl}.
It has four goals: to check that the kernel encoding costs the same as coefficient-form folding at identical engineering (as predicted by \cref{rem:basefold}), to measure the cost of the post-quantum parameters and of the salted compiler, to locate where the time goes and quantify the protocol parameters $\ell$ and $\rho$, and to place the implementation next to an independent multilinear commitment scheme.

\subsection{Setup}\label{sec:exp:setup}

\paragraph{Machine and code.}
All runs use a cloud virtual machine with an Intel Xeon processor at $2.10$\,GHz ($2$ vCPUs, $7$\,GB RAM), Rust $1.95.0$, release profile with fat LTO and one codegen unit, and no \texttt{target-cpu} flag.
The library is \texttt{kbfold}~\kbversion; the script \texttt{rust/bench/run\_all.sh} runs every measurement of this section and writes the data of the tables to \texttt{rust/results/}.
Unless stated otherwise, runs are single-threaded.
Times are medians over $11$ runs for $n \le 16$, $5$ for $n \in \{18, 20\}$ and $3$ for $n = 22$ and for \cref{tab:stop,tab:rate}; verification times are medians over $21$ runs.
The machine is shared, and timings of identical work vary by up to about $20\%$ between runs (\cref{sec:exp:breakdown}).

\paragraph{Parameters.}
The \emph{classical parameters} are: rate $\rho = 1/4$ ($R = 2$), $\ell = n - 4$ folding rounds (final table of $16$ elements), $\kappa = 148$ queries, the quadratic extension $\K$, and $32$-byte salts.
By \cref{thm:sound} with $\delta = (1-\rho)/2 = 3/8$, the query term is $(5/8)^{148} < 2^{-100}$, and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{fold}} + 2\ell/|\K| < 2^{-100}$ for all sizes tested (\cref{rem:params}).
The \emph{post-quantum parameters} are those of \cref{rem:pq-params}: the same rate and $\ell$, $\kappa = 248$ queries and the quartic extension.
Tables $f$ are uniformly random over $\mathbb{F}_p$ and the point $z$ is uniformly random in $\F^n$.
Proof sizes are the lengths of the canonical encoding of \cref{sec:impl:merkle}.

\paragraph{Baseline.}
To isolate the effect of the encoding, the library has a second mode that differs from $\Pieval$ in exactly two places: the table is converted to the monomial coefficients of $\tilde f$ by M\"obius inversion (\cref{lem:mobius}) instead of the transform of \cref{cor:transforms}, and words are folded with the classical FRI fold $\mathrm{cfold}_\theta(w) = w_e + \theta\,w_o$ (\cref{def:cfold}) instead of the kernel fold (\cref{rem:coeff-form}).
This is the coefficient-form folding used in Reed--Solomon instantiations of BaseFold and in DeepFold; everything else (field, NTT, Merkle trees, transcript, sumcheck, query phase) is shared.
The baseline passes the same completeness and tampering tests.
It is \emph{not} the BaseFold or DeepFold code base, and \cref{tab:scaling} compares encodings, not implementations.

\Cref{tab:scaling} reports commit, open and verify times for $n = 12,\dots,22$ ($N = 2^{12},\dots,2^{22}$).
Proof sizes are identical because the proofs have the same shape.
The differences between the two encodings change sign from one size to the next (the kernel encoding is $15\%$ slower to open at $n = 14$ and $9\%$ faster to commit at $n = 22$), and they are of the same size as the differences in steps whose code is identical in both modes: in \cref{tab:breakdown} the NTT times differ by $17\%$ between modes although the computation is the same.
The two encodings are therefore indistinguishable at the resolution of this machine.
This is the outcome predicted by \cref{rem:basefold}: the kernel transform and M\"obius inversion both cost $\tfrac{n}{2}N$ additions, and in line form (\cref{lem:line}) the kernel fold costs one multiplication by $r$ per output element, the same as the classical fold.
The kernel encoding thus provides its structural property (\cref{thm:fold-dictionary}) at no measurable cost.

\Cref{tab:pq} compares the two parameter sets.
The post-quantum parameters cost $1.4$--$1.7$ times the prover time and $1.8$ times the proof size of the classical ones: the extension doubles the size of every folded value and of the sumcheck arithmetic, and the number of queries grows from $148$ to $248$.
Commitment is unchanged, since $w_0$ lives over $\mathbb{F}_p$ in both cases.

\paragraph{Salts.}
At $n = 20$ with the classical parameters, the prover takes $1214$\,ms with unsalted trees and $1268$\,ms with $32$-byte salts, a difference within the noise of the machine.
Salts add $74.5$\,KiB to the proof ($1072$ to $1147$\,KiB): one salt per opened leaf ($148$ queries, $16$ levels) and $17$ round salts.

\paragraph{Parallelism.}
With the feature \texttt{parallel}, the transforms, the NTT, the Merkle trees, the folds and the sumcheck run on all cores; the proofs are identical to the single-threaded ones, which the test vectors check.
On the two vCPUs of this machine, the prover time with the classical parameters drops from $1195$ to $786$\,ms at $n = 20$ and from $6171$ to $3737$\,ms at $n = 22$ (speed-ups $1.5$ and $1.65$), and with the post-quantum parameters from $2004$ to $1179$\,ms and from $9246$ to $5864$\,ms ($1.7$ and $1.6$).
The verifier stays single-threaded.

\caption{Components of the commitment in ms ($n = 20$, $M = 4N$, classical parameters, single-threaded), measured separately from \cref{tab:scaling}. The NTT and Merkle columns execute identical code in both modes.}
\label{tab:breakdown}
\end{table}

\Cref{tab:breakdown} splits the commitment into its three steps.
The kernel transform takes about $2\%$ of the commitment; it is slower than M\"obius inversion by $3.4$\,ms because it writes both entries of each pair, against one for M\"obius inversion, while both perform $\tfrac{n}{2}N$ additions.
The commitment is dominated by hashing: BLAKE3 over $M/2$ salted fibre leaves and $M/2 - 1$ internal nodes takes $2$ to $2.5$ times as long as the NTT.
Opening costs about as much as committing: the folded words $w_1,\dots,w_{\ell-1}$ have total length below $M$ but live in $\F$, and each needs its own Merkle tree; the sumcheck ($O(N)$ multiplications in $\F$) is a minor term.

Verification takes $1.6$--$4.6$\,ms with the classical parameters and grows slowly with $n$, as expected from its $O(\kappa \ell \log M)$ hashing cost.
Proof sizes ($0.4$--$1.4$\,MiB) are dominated by the $\kappa \ell$ authentication paths: each query opens one leaf per level, and paths are not merged across queries (\cref{sec:concl:future}).

\Cref{tab:stop} varies $\ell$ at $n = 20$.
Each folding round adds $\kappa$ authentication paths and salts to the proof, while stopping earlier adds $16 N_\ell$ bytes for the final table; in this range the paths dominate, so proofs shrink as $\ell$ decreases, from $1249$\,KiB at $\ell = n$ to $784$\,KiB at $\ell = n-12$.
Verification time is smallest around $\ell = 12$: for smaller $\ell$ the verifier's $\kappa$ evaluations of $G$, each of cost $O(N_\ell)$, take over.
Opening time varies little with $\ell$, since the first folds dominate it.

\Cref{tab:rate} fixes the query term of \cref{thm:sound} at $2^{-100}$ and varies the rate.

Within the unique-decoding analysis of this paper, $\rho = 1/4$ is a reasonable default.

WHIR~\cite{ACFY24} is a multilinear polynomial commitment scheme from Reed--Solomon proximity testing.
To place our implementation among existing systems, \cref{tab:whir} runs its implementation\footnote{Repository \url{https://github.com/WizardOfMenlo/whir}, commit \texttt{c03a4a5} of September 2026.} on the same machine with the same field, rate and security level, single-threaded, and without proof of work.
We use two configurations: folding one variable per round with the unique-decoding analysis, the setting closest to ours, and WHIR's default of four variables per round with its analysis up to the Johnson bound.
The two code bases differ in many engineering choices (field arithmetic, Merkle trees, transcript, proof encoding), so \cref{tab:whir} is a reference point, not a comparison of protocols.

On this machine the \texttt{kbfold} prover is $5$--$7$ times faster than WHIR with one variable per round and $1.3$--$2.2$ times faster than WHIR with four; WHIR's proofs are $2.1$--$2.6$ times smaller in the first configuration and $10$--$11.4$ times smaller in the second, where its verifier is also about $5$ times faster.
The difference in proof size comes from three techniques that are independent of the encoding: the analysis up to the Johnson bound, which reduces the number of queries; folding several variables per round, which reduces the number of levels and hence of authentication paths per query; and the encoding of openings.
Each of them applies to the kernel encoding, and they are part of the future work of \cref{sec:concl:future}.
